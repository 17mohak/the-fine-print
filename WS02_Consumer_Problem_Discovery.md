## 0. How to read this document

**No interviews were conducted. Nothing here is primary research.** Everything is desk research from web search results, mostly secondary (news coverage of regulator reports and online surveys). I did not open the primary PDFs (IRDAI, CIO, RBI, NCH). Every number below must be re-verified against the primary source before it goes into any Gate 2 decision.

Evidence tags used throughout:

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[REG]** | Regulator / government data (via press coverage, primary not yet checked) |
| **[SURVEY]** | Survey or study. Note sponsor and sampling. |
| **[CASE]** | Documented individual case (consumer commission ruling, reported complaint) |
| **[ANEC]** | Anecdote, viral post, blog |
| **[HYP]** | My hypothesis, untested |

Sample caveat: LocalCircles surveys are self-selected online respondents, not nationally representative. They show that a problem exists, not its national rate. CoverSure is an insurtech with a commercial interest in "people don't understand insurance".

## B. Domain comparison

Ratings are my judgement from the evidence above (H/M/L), with confidence noted. Rental and lending ratings rest on much thinner evidence.

| Dimension | Insurance (health/motor) | Rental agreements | Lending (personal/education/vehicle) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Severity of pain** | High. Hits at a medical or accident crisis; claim value can be lakhs. | Medium. Deposit loss is usually ₹30k–₹1L+ (anecdotal). | Medium–High, but mostly slow-burn (fees, rate resets). Severe if default. |
| **Frequency** | High among claimants: ~11% repudiated, ~6% pending, survey-reported partial payments far higher (self-selected). Fewer people claim in a given year. | Every renter exits once; disputes' rate unknown. **No rate data.** | Everyone borrowing is exposed; dispute rate unknown. |
| **Financial consequence** | High, lump-sum. | Low–Medium. | Medium, cumulative. |
| **Existing alternatives** | Many: insurer grievance, IRDAI Bima Bharosa, Insurance Ombudsman, Samadhan-type platforms, consumer commissions, agents/brokers, lawyers. | Landlord negotiation, legal notice, rent-agreement/property platforms (Housing.com, RentOk etc.); Model Tenancy Act adoption uneven. | RBI Ombudsman (CMS portal), bank grievance, KFS mandate. |
| **Accessibility of users** | Good: reachable via claim-rejection forums, Samadhan-type communities. Timing problem: pain peaks at the claim. | Good for renters in metros; short relationship per tenancy. | Moderate; sensitive to disclose. |
| **Accessibility of documents** | Good: standardised policy wording, IRDAI-mandated formats, one PDF. Claim correspondence is the harder part. | Poor: bespoke, often unregistered, photographed, sometimes oral. Wide variance. | Medium: sanction letter, KFS, schedule of charges; fragmented across lender systems. |
| **Trust barriers** | High: people already distrust insurers; 8 in 10 survey respondents suspect delay is deliberate. Would also distrust an unverified AI verdict on a claim. [HYP] | Medium. | High: financial data sensitivity. [HYP] |
| **Likely willingness to pay** | Medium–High at the claim moment (high stakes), but consumer WTP for software at a **pre-purchase** moment is unproven. [HYP] | Low–Medium; small stakes, one-off. [HYP] | Low–Medium; free regulator channels and comparison sites crowd it. [HYP] |
| **Evidence quality in this pass** | Strong (regulator + multiple surveys + cases). | **Weak** (anecdote and explainers only). | **Weak** for comprehension (volume data only). |

Reading: insurance is the best-evidenced domain by a distance. That is partly because it is better documented in public data, not necessarily because it is the best opportunity. Rental and lending are underevidenced here, not disproven.

## C. Behavioural profiles (evidence-backed where possible)

These are hypotheses assembled from the evidence; each must be tested in interviews (Section E).

1. **The Post-Rejection Claimant.** Has a claim rejected, partly paid, or stalled. Evidence: IRDAI repudiation data; LocalCircles 15%/29% invalid rejection/partial approval; Ombudsman volumes. Behaviour: compares the rejection letter to the policy, tries insurer grievance, then escalates or gives up. Unknown: share who ever escalate. High motivation, urgent, willing to engage.
2. **The Trusting Buyer.** Buys on agent or aggregator advice and does not read the wording. Evidence: only 35% say they fully understand (CoverSure, commercial). Reading behaviour itself is **not measured** in anything I found. Low motivation until a claim.
3. **The Hospital-Bed Decider.** Family member in hospital, needs cashless approval and discharge, not understanding. Evidence: 5 in 10 reported 6–48 hours to approval and discharge (LocalCircles); IRDAI circular targets 1 hr / 3 hr. [HYP] Needs real-time help, not document analysis.
4. **The Deposit-at-Risk Tenant.** Exiting a tenancy with a deposit at stake and no move-in record. Evidence: viral cases only. [HYP]
5. **The Passive Subscriber / Borrower.** Enrolled in recurring charges or loan fees without awareness. Evidence: 67% subscription-trap report (self-selected); lender fee variance. Needs surfacing and alerts, not explanation.
6. **The Delegator.** Relies on a family member, broker or lawyer. [HYP] Possibly the real buyer in many cases, which changes who the user is.

## D. Jobs to be done

(Phrased as what users try to accomplish; each is [HYP] until tested.)

- "When my claim is rejected, help me find out whether the reason is actually in my policy, so I can decide whether to fight." (Evidence it matters: commission cases overturning rejections on grounds not in the policy.)
- "When I'm about to buy, tell me what I will be annoyed to find out later" (exclusions, waiting periods, sub-limits, fees).
- "When someone is in hospital, tell me what I'm entitled to right now and what to say."
- "Make sure I don't miss the date that costs me money" (renewal, notice, 48-hour intimation, waiting-period end).
- "Give me the evidence pack and the right forum so I don't have to figure out the process" (insurer → Bima Bharosa/Ombudsman → commission).
- "Show me every recurring charge or obligation I've committed to."

Note: the charter's three pillars assume the jobs are linked. The evidence suggests the strongest job is dispute-stage (Act), and the weakest-evidenced is generic "Understand".

## E. Interview plan

**No interviews have been run.** This is a plan only.

**Recruitment (aim 24–30 across segments; stop per segment at saturation):**
- Health/motor claim experience in last 24 months: rejected, partly paid, delayed, and also smooth claims (≥5 each, to avoid selecting only aggrieved).
- Bought health or motor insurance in last 6 months, no claim yet (≥5).
- Signed or exited a rental in last 12 months (≥6, including some with no dispute).
- Took personal, education or vehicle loan in last 18 months; include co-borrowers/parents for education loans (≥6).
- Used a grievance channel (insurer, Ombudsman, consumer commission, helpline) (≥4).
- Include non-metro, vernacular-language, and non-English-reading participants. Exclude people who work in insurance, lending, legal-tech or have a stake in the product. Recruit outside the product's own channels to avoid selection toward people who already want a solution.
- Ethics: informed consent, no collection of actual policy documents unless explicit and redacted, record only with permission, honest incentive disclosure.

**Method for avoiding leading questions:**
- Ask about specific past events ("the last time…"), not opinions or hypotheticals.
- Do not mention AI, the product, or "fine print" until the final, separate segment.
- Open questions first; probe with "tell me more" and "what happened next?"; never offer answer lists.
- Ask for artefacts shown on screen, not summaries, to compare stated and actual behaviour.
- Neutral wording ("what did you do with the document" not "did you read it").
- Two interviewers; one codes verbatims independently; log disconfirming quotes separately.
- Treat WTP answers as weak; instead ask what they have already paid for (lawyers, agents, platforms).

**Questions (19):**
1. Tell me about the last time you bought/signed [policy / rental / loan]. Walk me through it from the first thought.
2. What documents did you receive? Where are they now? Show me if you can.
3. What did you do with them when they arrived?
4. Which parts, if any, did you look at, and why those?
5. Who or what did you rely on to decide? What did they tell you?
6. Was there anything you later found out that you wish you had known? How did you find out?
7. What, if anything, did you do differently the next time?
8. Tell me about a time something went wrong (claim, deposit, fee, cancellation). What happened first?
9. What did you do next, and after that? Who did you contact?
10. How long did it take from start to the current state? What did it cost you in money, time and other effort?
11. What documents or information were hard to find or missing when you needed them?
12. What did the other side (insurer / landlord / lender) tell you, and how did you check whether it was right?
13. Which sources did you believe or doubt, and what made you do so?
14. Did you consider the Ombudsman, a consumer commission, or a lawyer? What decided it?
15. If you gave up, what made you stop?
16. What do you do now to keep track of renewals, deadlines or charges?
17. What would have had to be true for you to have acted differently at signing?
18. (Only now) Here is a tool concept. Show a mock explanation; tell me what you'd check before trusting it, and what you would do with it.
19. What have you paid for help with this kind of thing before? What would stop you uploading your documents?

## F. Problem prioritisation — top three

1. **Disputed/rejected/partly paid health claims where the rejection basis is unclear or contestable.** Why: strongest evidence base (regulator volumes, 95% of Ombudsman health complaints are denials, repeated commission reversals), high stakes, defined next steps and forums. Caveat: reversals in commissions are a selected sample; many rejections are valid.
2. **Not knowing what's covered and excluded before you need it (insurance pre-purchase and mid-policy).** Why: widespread self-reported low understanding. Caveat: the evidence is a commercially sponsored survey; self-reported understanding is not measured comprehension, and motivation to act before a claim is unproven. Possibly better as an on-ramp to #1 than a standalone product.
3. **Recurring charges and obligations people can't see or cancel (subscriptions, fees, renewals).** Why: high prevalence in surveys, clear action (cancel/alert). Caveat: self-selected surveys, low per-incident stakes, and crowded with bank and UPI controls and a regulatory regime (CCPA dark-pattern guidelines).

Not selected for lack of evidence, not for lack of pain: rental deposit disputes and loan-term comprehension. A proper Gate 1 would add targeted evidence for both before ruling them out.

## G. Disconfirming evidence — why consumers may not want this

- **The problem may be real but the product may not be the fix.** Claims fail mostly for non-document reasons: insurer behaviour, hospital paperwork, delay. Understanding the policy does not change an insurer's decision. [HYP]
- **Free or incumbent paths exist.** Insurer grievance, IRDAI Bima Bharosa, Ombudsman (low-cost), RBI Ombudsman, National Consumer Helpline, claim-assistance firms and agents. Whether consumers find these inadequate or just unknown is unmeasured.
- **Reading behaviour is not proven.** Surveys show low self-reported understanding; none found measure whether people would read an explanation. People may prefer to delegate to an agent or relative. [HYP]
- **Timing mismatch.** Pain is acute at claim time, but people only seek help then, which suggests an episodic, low-retention product with hard-to-sustain subscription revenue. [HYP]
- **Regulation is already moving.** IRDAI cut the moratorium to 5 years and set 1-hour/3-hour cashless timelines; KFS rules for loans; CCPA dark-pattern guidelines. Some of the problem may shrink. Conversely, the data show rejections and complaints still rising in FY24–25.
- **Survey bias.** The strongest-sounding numbers (50% rejected/partial, 8 in 10 suspect deliberate delay) come from self-selected online panels that over-represent the aggrieved. Regulator data (~11% repudiated) is the safer baseline.
- **Trust.** Users may not accept an AI interpretation of their claim, or may not upload sensitive documents. Valid concern given the charter's own principle against presenting probabilistic output as a legal determination.
- **Liability and credibility.** If the tool is wrong about a claim, the user's loss is concrete. Unlicensed-advice risk applies.
- **Willingness to pay is unevidenced.** No source found tests payment for this kind of product. Evidence on consumer spend is anecdotal at best.
- **Courts are slow.** Even a good evidence pack leads into a backlogged system (NCDRC 18,767 pending; 5.43 lakh overall). That cuts both ways: it raises the need for pre-litigation resolution, and it lowers users' expectation that effort pays off.

**Bottom line:** There is a documented, large, and growing claims-dispute problem in Indian health insurance. That is not yet a viable product. Viability depends on three untested things: whether document-grounded analysis changes outcomes, whether users will pay at the moment of need, and whether trust and liability can be managed.
